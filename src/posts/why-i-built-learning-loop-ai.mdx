*How my experience teaching programming inspired me to build an AI-powered learning platform, and what developing it taught me about software engineering.*

[Try Learning Loop AI](https://learning-loop-ai-alpha.vercel.app/) · [View the source code](https://github.com/Mela13076/learning-loop-ai)

## An Idea I've Had for Years

For the past several years, I've spent a lot of time helping people learn programming. During college, I worked as a tutor and teaching assistant. Today, I teach kids how to program.

Throughout those experiences, I've noticed something that has stuck with me: understanding a programming concept and knowing how to apply it are two different things. A student might understand what an `if` statement does or how a `for` loop works. But give them a problem and ask them to write the code themselves, and they sometimes don't know where to start.

Usually, we step away from the project for a moment. I'll explain the concept again, walk through a smaller example, and have them break the problem into steps before writing any code. Over time, I've seen how much that combination of guidance, practice, and repetition matters.

My first idea was simple: build an application that generates quizzes with AI so students can practice programming concepts and check what they understand. I called that early project [CodeAgent](/blogs/codeagent-progress-part-1). As I thought more about how my students learn, the idea grew into **Learning Loop AI**.

<hr />

## Why Quizzes Weren't Enough

Quizzes are a great way to check what someone knows, but what happens when they don't know the answer?

I've seen students get stuck on questions they have the knowledge to solve. Sometimes they need a reminder of a concept. Other times, they need a smaller example or someone to ask a question that helps them take the next step. Knowing an answer is wrong doesn't always tell you how to get unstuck.

That's where the idea of a learning loop took shape. I wanted students to be able to learn a concept, practice it, get feedback, and return to the parts they struggled with. The goal is to give learners a way to keep making progress between lessons, when a teacher isn't there to guide every step.

I go through that same process when I'm learning a new technology or practicing piano. There's something motivating about returning to a concept you once struggled with and realizing you can finally use it. I wanted to help learners experience that progress for themselves.

## Turning the Idea Into a Working Application

I chose tools I already knew so I could focus on the learning experience and the challenges of integrating AI. I built the application with **Next.js, React, and TypeScript**, using **PostgreSQL and Prisma** to manage learning paths, topics, study sessions, quiz attempts, and user progress. I used **Clerk** for authentication and **Gemini** for the AI features.

The first version includes learning paths, an AI coach, generated quizzes, study timers, notes, and a dashboard with progress tracking and study recommendations. Learners can generate a quiz for a topic they're studying and get feedback explaining why an answer was wrong. Their results also feed into progress tracking.

I wanted learners to finish a quiz with a better idea of what to practice next. The score is useful, but the feedback is what helps them take that next step.

## Making AI Useful, Not Just Available

One of the biggest decisions I made was how the AI coach should interact with students. I could have built a chatbot that answers any programming question. But in teaching, I've seen how easy it is to get used to receiving a solution before giving yourself a chance to work through it.

I designed the coach around guided interactions. Learners can ask for an explanation, an example, a hint, or a quiz tied to what they're studying. I want the coach to help them think through a problem in the way an instructor might.

Building those features taught me that generating content is only part of integrating AI. A generated quiz still needs consistent question structures, valid answers, and predictable grading. The app has to handle malformed responses and failures without losing a learner's work or incorrectly updating their progress.

I separated AI interactions from the rest of the application logic, supported both mock and real AI responses, and added tests around quiz structure and grading. **AI can generate the content, but the application is responsible for validating it and deciding how to use it.**

## Thinking Beyond Scores and Study Time

Designing the mastery system was another engineering challenge. At first, measuring progress with quiz scores and study time seemed reasonable. But spending an hour studying doesn't necessarily mean someone understands a topic.

The current system combines quiz performance, repeated practice, study time, and concept coverage. It also requires learners to pass an advanced quiz before a topic can be considered mastered.

It's a starting point. Eventually, I want mastery to reflect whether learners can apply a concept independently, especially when a problem looks different from the examples they've practiced.

## What I'm Building Next

In the first version, learners can review concepts and follow links to outside resources. I originally thought those resources would encourage them to explore independently. Feedback made me reconsider how much of the learning experience should happen inside the application.

For Version 2, I'm planning built-in modules that explain concepts, demonstrate examples, ask learners to predict outcomes, and give them a chance to practice. I also want to build a Python coding lab so learners can write and test code in the app. More advanced hints and personalized review based on earlier mistakes are also on the roadmap.

I want those features to work together: a learner should be able to study a concept, try it in code, get help when stuck, and revisit it later with a better idea of what to practice.

## What This Project Taught Me as a Developer

Learning Loop AI is one of the largest full-stack projects I've built independently. It pushed me to think beyond individual features and pay more attention to architecture, data relationships, testing, and how I would maintain the application as it grew.

It also changed how I use AI during development. I use it to research technologies, think through architectural decisions, and move faster on implementation. I still want to understand why a decision makes sense and what trade-offs I'm accepting.

**AI can build amazing things, but so can I.**

That mindset matters to me both as a developer and as a teacher. I want Learning Loop AI to reflect what I've learned from working with students: good guidance gives them room to think for themselves. I'm proud that I took something I'd seen for years while teaching and built a working application around it. There's plenty more to build, but the goal is still the same: help beginners become more confident and independent problem-solvers.
